The finisher who has captained his country

Rovman Powell's T20 career is defined by innings that should not have been possible. His unbeaten 107 off 53 balls against England in 2022, when West Indies chased 172 with an over to spare, is the template: he arrived with the game drifting, hit ten sixes, and finished it himself. He has captained West Indies in T20 internationals, led Jamaica in the Caribbean Premier League, and played in the IPL for Delhi Capitals and Kolkata Knight Riders, where his finishing has been his calling card.

  • Role: Batter
  • Team: Dubai Capitals
  • Batting: Right-handed
  • Bowling: Right-arm medium-fast
  • Born: 23 July 1993

He was one of four players the Capitals pre-signed before the auction, alongside Stoinis, Mustafizur and Noor Ahmad. That tells you how the champions see him: not as a squad player, but as a core piece of a title-winning side. Finishers of his quality are the hardest players to find in T20 cricket, and the Capitals did not want to risk the auction.

The last five overs

Powell's job is the last five overs, where his power and his calm decide games. He bats at five or six, behind Stoinis and Shanaka, and his brief is to take the innings from a competitive total to a winning one — or to chase down a target that looks out of reach. His bowling is a bonus: medium pace that can fill an over or two when the captain needs it, and a safe pair of hands in the deep.

In a middle order with Stoinis and Shanaka, Powell is the purest hitter of the three. Du Plessis will use him as the closer: the player who walks in with 40 needed off 18 balls and makes it look routine. His record says he does that more often than not.

Three finishers, no escape

The Capitals' middle order is the deepest in the league: Stoinis, Shanaka and Powell can each win a match from a losing position, and Karim Janat adds a fourth option behind them. That depth changes how the opposition bowls — there is no over to hide in, no bowler to target, because the next batter hits just as hard as the last one. It also changes how the top order plays: Salt and du Plessis can attack the powerplay knowing a collapse does not end the innings.
