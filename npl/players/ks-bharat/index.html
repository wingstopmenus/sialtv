Season 3 summary

KS Bharat's signing was, on paper, one of the signings of the season. The India wicketkeeper — full name Kona Srikar Bharat, born 3 October 1993 in Visakhapatnam — arrived at Kathmandu Gorkhas as an overseas signing alongside David Warner and Sahan Arachchige, a right-hand batter with a first-class technique and a Ranji Trophy triple hundred behind him. He has played Test cricket for India. He has more than 1,800 T20 runs.

Five matches into NPL Season 3, he has no batting or bowling entry in the stats file. He has not batted in any of Kathmandu's five league matches, and he has not bowled. The gloves, meanwhile, have belonged to Sahil Patel, the Nepali keeper, who has batted in the last three matches — 10 against Biratnagar, 38 against Lumbini, 8 against Chitwan — and kept wicket in the wins that followed.

It is the classic overseas-signing squeeze. A T20 side plays four overseas players at most, and Kathmandu's three — Warner, Bharat and Arachchige — were competing for places in an XI that also had to carry its domestic core. Warner took one spot at the top of the order and delivered 127 runs. Arachchige took another and delivered with bat and ball. Bharat, the keeper, lost his spot to a domestic keeper who was already in form — and when a side wins four out of five, the XI does not change.

Player profile

Bharat is a right-hand wicketkeeper-batter whose game is built on technique rather than power. He is compact, still-headed, and strongest against spin on subcontinent surfaces: the Ranji triple hundred was an innings of accumulation and placement, and that game translates to T20 middle overs, where he manipulates fields and finds boundaries without taking undue risks. He is not a power-hitter in the modern finisher mould — his strength is control, and control is what wins tight chases.

That profile is exactly what Kathmandu's middle order has lacked at times. The Gorkhas' batting has been top-heavy: Warner at the top, Rashid Khan and Karan KC in the middle, and then a long tail. In the opener against Janakpur, Kathmandu were bowled out for 115. In the Lumbini win, they made 160 for 8. A keeper-batter who can bat at five or six and hold an innings together is a different kind of insurance from the hitters around him.

His first-class record is the evidence. A triple hundred in the Ranji Trophy does not happen by accident — it takes concentration across two days, and the technique to survive the new ball and then dominate the old one. The T20 numbers, more than 1,800 runs, show the white-ball game is there too. The question for Kathmandu is not whether Bharat can play. It is whether they need him to.

Debut wait

David Warner flew home on 10 November, and his departure changes the equation. Kathmandu lose their opener and their biggest name, and the side that finishes the season will look different from the side that started it. Two league matches remain — Sudurpaschim Royals on Friday 13 November and Pokhara Avengers on Sunday 15 November — and then the playoffs, starting with Qualifier 1 on Tuesday 17 November for the top two.

An overseas slot has opened, and Bharat is the most experienced player in the squad still waiting. Whether he takes it depends on what the captain and coach want: a keeper-batter to steady the middle order, or another hitter to replace Warner's intent at the top. Sahil Patel has kept well and batted usefully, so the gloves are not obviously Bharat's to take — but as a batter alone, his record argues for a place.

There is also the simpler reading: five matches, four wins, top of the table. A side in that position does not change for the sake of it. Bharat's chance, if it comes, will come because Kathmandu decide they need his calm in a big match — a playoff, a chase, a night when the hitters have failed. Until then, he remains the most accomplished player in the NPL who has not yet played a ball.
